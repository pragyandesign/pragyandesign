Project context

Photographers were doing the invoicing a product should have done for them

[PLACEHOLDER narrative] Freelance photographers using Aperture to manage bookings had no way to collect a deposit at the point a client confirmed a shoot. Every booking turned into a second, manual job: drafting an invoice, sending a payment link, following up when it went unpaid, and rescheduling around clients who never fully committed. The loss wasn't just time — unconfirmed bookings held calendar slots that a paying client could have taken.

Key design moments

Three calls, and what I didn't pick

Hiring research keeps landing on the same point: the options you rejected, and why, show more judgement than the one you shipped.

1

When should payment enter the flow?

The old flow asked for payment only after the shoot was completed.

RejectedInvoice after the shoot

Matches how photographers already worked, but kept the exact chasing problem the project was meant to fix.

RejectedFull payment up front

Solved the money problem but tested badly — clients balked at paying in full before meeting the photographer.

ShippedDeposit at confirmation, balance at delivery

Asks for commitment at the moment intent is highest — right after the client picks a date.

Chosen because the deposit only has to be large enough to make walking away costly, not large enough to fund the shoot — that reframing is what got it past client objections in testing.

2

What does the photographer see when a client stalls?

Clients who hadn't paid the deposit still held the calendar slot.

RejectedSilent auto-cancel after 48 hours

Simple to build, but removed the photographer's judgement entirely — some stalls are a client on holiday, not a lost booking.

Shipped"At risk" status + one-tap nudge, auto-release at 5 days

Puts the decision back with the photographer first, with a safety net if nobody acts.

Chosen because the person who knows the client should keep the authority to decide — the system's job is to surface the risk early, not to act on it alone.

3

How much payment infrastructure to build for v1?

Refunds, partial refunds and disputes are all real cases.

RejectedBuild the full refund/dispute system

More complete, but would have taken the whole seven weeks before a single real booking could be tested.

ShippedDeposit flow only, refunds handled manually by the processor's dashboard

A narrower, testable slice — the core trust mechanism, not the whole payments surface.

Chosen because a working narrow flow in front of real photographers taught more in week seven than a wider, untested one would have.

Reflections

What I'd keep, what I'd change, what surprised me

What I'd keep

Putting the authority to act on a stalled client back with the photographer instead of automating it away. The one-tap nudge tested better than any clever automatic rule I tried first.

What I'd change

[PLACEHOLDER] I'd test the deposit percentage itself, not just the flow around it — I picked 20% by instinct and never validated it against what actually changes a client's behaviour.

What surprised me

[PLACEHOLDER] Photographers cared less about collecting the money and more about not being the one who had to ask for it. The design problem was partly emotional, not just operational.
