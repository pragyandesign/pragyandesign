ClearFleet

I found a flaw in my own design that could have hurt someone — and that changed how I design now.

Role
Solo product designer — research, IA, UX, visual design, brand identity
Platforms
Web dashboard (dispatcher) · iOS app (driver)
Tools
Figma, Claude and Cursor
Research
Secondary research only — no primary interviews, stated plainly
Scope
9 screens across dispatcher web and driver mobile, plus a landing page
The core loop
A stop fails → it becomes an exception → the dispatcher resolves it
Project context

Dispatchers running a 20–100 vehicle fleet had no way to know what was happening without calling

Dispatchers default to calling drivers directly — dozens of times a shift — just to stay informed. A single delivery can generate up to 7–8 check calls for status updates alone, cited from logistics industry research, not invented for this page.

  • Phone calls are a symptom of poor ground visibility, not the root problem — dispatchers call because they have no other way to know fleet status.
  • Exceptions aren't one bucket. Breakdown, wrong address and delay each need a genuinely different resolution path.
  • No record, no accountability. Without a log of how an issue was resolved, disputes come down to memory.
A number that was cut, on purpose: an early draft claimed dispatchers make 50–80 calls a shift. It couldn't be re-verified against a real source, so it was removed and replaced with the citable 7–8 check-calls figure above — less dramatic, but defensible.
In scope: dispatcher web, driver mobile, and a marketing landing page Out of scope on purpose: route optimization, billing, driver onboarding Context profiles built from role and environment, not interview-based personas
Final artifacts

Nine screens, each earning a specific job

The dispatcher's whole reason for existing is awareness; the driver's is speed. Scroll a stack to see every screen in it — the notes beside it stay in place.

Dispatcher web app

Desk-bound, interruption-driven work. Built for scanning a wide screen, not tapping a phone.

  1. Live Fleet ViewDefault home screen — no menu, no landing page. Awareness first.
  2. Route AssignmentEvery field needed to decide sits on one screen, including an inline zone-mismatch warning.
  3. Exception QueueEvery problem lands here, sorted by what needs attention now.
  4. Driver / Route DetailWhere a breakdown actually gets worked — route and driver safety, both resolved here.
  5. Day SummaryOne completion-rate number tells the story first; everything below explains why.

Driver iOS app

Mobile by definition — in the vehicle, walking to doors, often one-handed. One clear next action at a time.

  1. Today's RouteShows only the next stop. Glanced at between stops, not studied.
  2. Report BreakdownAuto-filled location and stop count — reported in three taps.
  3. Update Stop OutcomeA two-state toggle. Reason and photo fields appear only once Failed is selected.
  4. Update Stop Outcome — FailedRequired reason plus a required photo — no clutter on the success path.
  5. Delivery ConfirmationCloses a real interaction gap — the driver finally gets feedback the action registered.
Key design moments

Two calls, and what I didn't pick

One of these is the flaw this case study opens with — caught by cross-checking the flow against itself, not by a reviewer.

1

When is a breakdown actually resolved?

A breakdown exception needs to close eventually — but what actually counts as "closed"?

RejectedAuto-close once all stops are reassigned

Let a dispatcher mark a stranded driver "handled" just by reassigning their stops. A real safety gap, not a UX nitpick.

RejectedManual close only

Correct in theory, but leaves every breakdown sitting open with no system support to track it.

ShippedTwo independent states, both required

Route status and driver-safety status track separately — both must be true to close.

Chosen because a dispatcher could otherwise close a breakdown without ever confirming the driver was safe. Found while cross-checking the flow against itself, then rebuilt before this reached a single developer.

2

Zone-mismatch warning — block or inform?

Assigning a stop outside a driver's zone is sometimes wrong, sometimes deliberate.

RejectedConfirmation modal, blocks until dismissed

Alert fatigue — a dispatcher sees this scenario 10+ times a shift; a modal seen that often stops being read.

ShippedInline warning, never blocking

A small flag next to the mismatched driver. Visible, never in the way.

Chosen because zone mismatches are routine, not rare — a warning seen constantly is worse than no warning at all.

Impact

What changed — honestly framed

This is a concept project with no live users. The two groups of numbers below are kept deliberately separate so neither gets mistaken for the other.

Proven in process

  • A real safety gap caught and fixed — an early version could mark a stranded driver "handled" just by reassigning their stops.
  • Every screen cross-checked against every other — real data contradictions (timestamps, exception counts, zone mismatches, frozen driver progress) caught and corrected before this reached a developer.
  • An unverifiable research stat pulled from the landing page rather than left uncredited.

Reflections

What I'd keep, what I'd change, what's still open

What held up

The exception-resolution model — built once, found a real flaw in my own logic, rebuilt it before shipping.

What I'd do differently

Talk to one real dispatcher before writing the insight the whole product was built around. This stayed secondary research only, and I say so rather than imply otherwise.

What's still open

The business model — where fleet data actually comes from, who pays, and what the minimum viable customer looks like. Flagged by outside review, not yet answered.
